A **PIM** (Product Information Management) is software that collects, organises and enriches all product information in one place, and distributes it to every sales channel: ecommerce site, marketplaces, catalogues, B2B portals. In practice it's the **single source of truth** for products.

### The problem it solves

Without a PIM, product information is scattered: codes and prices in the ERP, descriptions in Word files, supplier spec sheets in PDFs and spreadsheets, photos in shared folders, translations in emails with the agency. Each channel is updated by hand, each in its own way.

The consequences show:

- **Incomplete or inconsistent listings** across the website, Amazon and the printed catalogue.
- **Slow launches**: a new collection takes weeks to go live, because data has to be chased and copied over.
- **Costly mistakes**: wrong sizes, missing materials, outdated images, and therefore returns and complaints.
- **Unmanageable translations** once there are three, five, ten languages.
- **Nobody knows which version** of a piece of data is right.

A PIM brings order: every piece of data has a home and an owner, and from there it goes everywhere.

### What a PIM manages

- **Attributes**: every product characteristic (material, size, weight, colour, care instructions, certifications), organised into product **families**. A T-shirt and a sofa have different attributes.
- **Variants**: sizes, colours and combinations, with shared data written only once.
- **Categories and classifications**, which can differ by channel (the site's tree isn't Amazon's).
- **Media**: photos, videos, spec sheets and manuals linked to the product. Many PIMs include a **DAM** (Digital Asset Management) or connect to an external one.
- **Translations and localisation**: copy per language, units of measure per country.
- **Completeness**: for each channel you define what's mandatory, and the PIM shows as a percentage what's missing. A product goes live only when it's complete.
- **Workflows**: who enters, who reviews, who approves.
- **Channels**: the same product can have a short title for the site, a longer one for Amazon and different copy for the B2B catalogue.

### PIM, ERP, DAM and Shopify: who does what

| | ERP | PIM | DAM | Shopify |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Question it answers | How many do I have and at what price? | What is it and how do I describe it? | Where are the photos and videos? | How do I sell it online? |
| Typical data | Codes, prices, stock, costs, suppliers | Descriptions, attributes, categories, translations | Images, videos, documents | Product pages, cart, orders, customers |
| Who uses it | Finance, logistics | Marketing, product managers | Designers, photographers, marketing | Ecommerce, marketing |
| Strength | Business processes | Data quality and consistency | File management | Selling and conversion |

The golden rule: **every piece of data has exactly one owner**. The base price originates in the ERP, the description in the PIM, the photo in the DAM, and Shopify receives them. If the same data can be edited in two places, sooner or later the two places stop matching.

### Examples of PIMs

| | Akeneo | Pimcore | Salsify | Plytix | inriver |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Model | SaaS, plus an open source edition | Open source, also as an enterprise edition | SaaS | SaaS | SaaS |
| For | Mid-size and large companies, ecommerce and retail | Companies with highly custom needs | Brands selling through large retailers | SMBs and growing brands | Manufacturers and enterprises |
| Strength | Ease of use, connector ecosystem | PIM, DAM and data management in one fully modifiable platform | Syndicating data to retailers and marketplaces | Affordable, quick to start | Complex product models, B2B |
| Watch out for | SaaS edition costs | Needs developers to configure | Cost, US-market focus | Less suited to very complex catalogues | Cost and project length |

- **Akeneo** is probably the most widespread PIM in ecommerce. The open source edition (Community) is a good starting point, while the SaaS edition adds workflows, media management and a connector marketplace.
- **Pimcore** isn't just a PIM: it combines data management, DAM and CMS. It's very powerful and flexible, but it's more a "framework" to configure than a ready-made product. A few examples where it shines:
  - a **manufacturer of technical components** with tens of thousands of items, spec sheets, certifications and compatibility between products, feeding a B2B portal and a Shopify store for consumers;
  - a **food company** that manages ingredients, nutritional values and allergens with different rules for each country, and generates labels and spec sheets automatically;
  - a **multi-brand group** using a single Pimcore to feed several Shopify stores, one per brand or country;
  - a **furniture maker** with configurable products (fabrics, finishes, sizes) and thousands of images managed in the built-in DAM.

  I cover it in more detail in [Pimcore and Shopify: how to integrate them]({% post_url en/2026-10-15-pimcore-shopify %}).
- **Salsify** is built for brands that must send product data to many retailers and marketplaces, each with its own requirements.
- **Plytix** is the typical choice for a catalogue of a few hundred to a few thousand products that needs to start quickly on a small budget.
- **inriver** is strong in B2B and with technical products that have many relationships between items (spare parts, accessories, compatibility).

### How a PIM integrates with Shopify

Shopify already has its own way of describing products, so integrating a PIM mostly means **translating** the PIM's model into Shopify's and keeping the two in sync.

{% include diagrams/pim-shopify.html %}

#### What becomes what

| In the PIM | In Shopify |
| --- | --- |
| Product family and attributes | Product type and **metafields** (custom fields: material, fit, dimensions) |
| Model with variants (size, colour) | Product with **options** and **variants** |
| Categories | **Collections** (or tags) |
| Images and videos | Product **media**, with variant-specific photos |
| Translations | Per-language translations, managed with **Shopify Markets** |
| Complex structured data (materials, designers, certifications) | **Metaobjects**, reusable across products |
| "Shopify" channel and completeness | Only complete products get published |

#### Three ways to connect them

1. **Ready-made connector**: many PIMs (Akeneo, Plytix, Salsify and others) have Shopify connectors, official or from partners. They're configured, not developed. It's the fastest option when the catalogue is standard.
2. **Integration platform (iPaaS)**: tools like Alumio, Make or Celigo connect PIM, ERP and Shopify with configurable, monitorable flows. Useful when more than two systems are involved.
3. **Custom integration**: an app or middleware built for the purpose, reading from the PIM and writing to Shopify through its APIs. Needed when the rules are unusual: pricing logic, bundles, configurators, several Shopify stores to feed, or a custom-built PIM like Pimcore.

#### A concrete example

A fashion brand sells in Italy, Germany and France, with about 3,000 products and two collections a year.

1. **ERP**: the product team creates new items with code, sizes, colours and list price.
2. **PIM (Akeneo)**: the codes arrive automatically. Marketing adds descriptions, materials, care tips and fit; the photo studio uploads images; the agency translates into German and French.
3. **Completeness**: when a product reaches 100% for the "Shopify" channel in all three languages, it's ready.
4. **Connector**: creates the product on Shopify with variants, metafields, images and translations, initially as a **draft**, and adds it to the right collections.
5. **Launch**: the ecommerce team reviews the drafts and publishes the collection on the planned date.
6. **Every day**: stock arrives from the ERP every few minutes, copy changes flow automatically from the PIM, and Shopify orders go back to the ERP for shipping and invoicing.

The result: a collection goes live in days instead of weeks, with identical listings in every language and no copy-paste.

#### Mistakes to avoid

- **Editing on Shopify the data that comes from the PIM**: the next sync overwrites it. Decide and communicate who edits what, and where.
- **Routing stock through the PIM**: it changes too often. It must go from the ERP (or warehouse system) straight to Shopify.
- **Ignoring Shopify's limits**: a product has at most three options (for example size, colour, material). A PIM model with more axes must be rethought before the integration, not during it.
- **Syncing everything every time**: with large catalogues it's slow and runs into API limits. Send only what changed.
- **Changing product URLs on every update**: you lose your Google rankings. A page's address should be set once and kept stable, or handled with redirects.
- **Forgetting about deletions**: what happens on Shopify when a product is discontinued in the PIM? Archiving is almost always better than deleting.

### When you need a PIM (and when you don't)

**You probably need one** if:

- you have more than a few hundred products, with many attributes;
- you sell on several channels (website, marketplaces, B2B, catalogues);
- you sell in several languages or countries;
- several people or departments work on product data;
- you receive supplier data in different formats;
- getting a product online takes too long, or listings often contain errors.

**You probably don't need one yet** if you have a small catalogue, a single channel and a single language. In that case the Shopify admin, with well-organised metafields and metaobjects, already does a great job as a "light PIM". The right time to introduce a PIM is before the mess becomes a cost, not after.
